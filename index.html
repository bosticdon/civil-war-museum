Sit for Your Tintype Portrait

During the Civil War, a soldier could walk into a traveling photographer's tent and have his picture made on a thin sheet of iron coated in black lacquer. These tintypes were cheap and tough enough to mail home, so thousands of soldiers sent one to their families before marching off to war.

The pictures were made with the wet-plate process. The plate had to be coated, exposed, and developed within minutes, and the exposure could take several seconds. Sitters were told not to move, and an iron head brace behind the chair helped them hold still. That is one reason so few people smiled in old photographs.

A tintype is also a mirror image: the camera recorded the scene directly on the plate, so left and right are reversed, just like your portrait here.
